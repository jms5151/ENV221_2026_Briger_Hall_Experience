The Commons Visualization Lab

The Commons Visualization Lab is a room that contains an enormous high-resolution LED video wall, featuring about 83 million pixels organized in a curved design to completely immerse the viewer.

1 Find it

Where to find it: This lab can be found on the ground floor of the Commons area of Briger Hall, just under the Commons Library. If starting from central atrium, go through the east hallway, past the glowing globe display, until you reach the large opening with the cafe, open seating, and large classrooms and conference rooms. Head in the direction of the staircase that leads to the library, and you should see the Visualization Lab, with its large screen observable through the glass walls.

3 What if / What might change?

Hypothetical alternative

A rotating exhibition that is open for the public to explore and learn. This alternative could also be a platform to showcase new research efforts, groups, and discoveries, while ensuring that there is a continuously recurring flow of people who would want to see the new exhibition. That being said, with this alternative, the space would cease being effective as a technical tool, and be more of an education and entertainment platform catering to a wider audience.

For the environment: An always-open exhibition could build public understanding of environmental research. Seeing curated environmental exhibitions could make these issues feel more concrete, and visitors who learn more may be more likely to support research, back policy changes, or change their own habits. This is a long-term, indirect benefit that depends on exhibits actually changing what visitors know and do. [2]

For people: Today, using the lab requires a reservation and a consultation, and only Princeton affiliates can book it. A rotating, always-open exhibition would remove that barrier, letting students, visitors, and the general public experience it without planning ahead. New exhibitions could showcase recent research and student groups, giving people a reason to keep coming back. The tradeoff is that researchers would lose a flexible working tool: if the wall is committed to exhibits, it is less available for lab meetings, data analysis, and classes, which are the uses it was designed for.

What we do not know: We don’t know what this would look like in practice, at a granular level. We don’t know how expensive it would be to constantly have an exhibition on rotation, nor do we know how much traction it would actually generate on a daily basis.

Stand at the glass wall outside the Commons Visualization Lab, on the ground floor of Briger Hall, just under the Commons Library. Look through the glass and find the screen. Notice how it doesn’t sit flat on the wall. It curves around the back of the room. Then look up at the ceiling tiles and down at the three long rows of tables, all facing the screen. Everything in this room is arranged around that one wall.

That wall is called the CURVE. It’s about thirty-nine feet wide and almost nine feet tall, with roughly eighty-three million pixels. That’s enough to show huge datasets one pixel for one data point, or to put several climate simulations or satellite maps side by side without shrinking any of them. If you stand at the center of the curve, every panel is the same distance from your eyes, which is why 3D scenes work so well here. The lab is run by Princeton Research Computing, and Princeton affiliates can book it after a consultation with the visualization team.

So here’s our question: how does giving a community access to a tool like this create environmental impact? The screen doesn’t reduce emissions by itself. The impact is indirect. It helps people understand complicated environmental data, and people who understand a problem are more likely to research it, vote on it, or change what they do.

Now imagine something different. This is a hypothetical. Suppose the lab were an always-open rotating exhibition instead of a room you have to book. Anyone walking through Briger Hall could see new research on the screen, not just Princeton affiliates with a reservation. More people would see environmental data, and the exhibits could showcase new research groups and discoveries.

But there are tradeoffs. Researchers would lose a private workspace for their data and meetings. The room would also become less of a technical tool and more of an educational display. We’re trading deep use by a few people for shallow exposure to many.

And here’s what we honestly don’t know. We don’t know what a constantly rotating exhibition would cost to run, or how many people would actually stop and watch. We also don’t know how much energy an eighty-three-million-pixel screen uses, and that would matter for any environmental comparison.

So here’s a question to take with you. Look at the screen again. If this wall were open to everyone all day, what’s the one thing you would want it to show?
