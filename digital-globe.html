Briger Hall Discovery Guide

Digital Globe

The Dynamic Earth Visualization Globe, located on the ground floor of Princeton University's Briger Hall, is a digital display that brings Earth's natural processes to life. It can show everything from global weather patterns and ocean currents to earthquakes and changes in temperature. Rather than simply showing what our planet looks like, it helps visitors understand how Earth is constantly changing.

1 Find it

Where to find it: The globe is located on the ground floor of Briger Hall, in the hallway connecting buildings B and C. It is to the right of several gemstone displays, and a model of a dinosaur head.

Look closely: You will notice the bright red and orange colors covering much of the globe, indicating warm ocean temperatures especially around the Pacific Ocean. Notice how the colors contrast with the yellow regions farther north and south, showing cooler waters. You may also notice the clouds shifting over the water depicting weather phenomena at the same time. These differences represent the kinds of environmental patterns the globe can visualize, showing how scientific data can reveal changes across different parts of Earth.

2 Why is it interesting?

Our observation: The digital globe is capable of displaying seismic activity in addition to weather patterns. One display showed seismic waves emanating from an earthquake originating in Chile and spreading across the American continents.

Data for earthquakes like these were simulated using the SPECFEM3D_GLOBE software. Other programs are also able to display ocean currents on Earth or rover landings on Mars using locational data and historic recordings. https://www.linkedin.com/posts/fjsimons_surface-waves-from-the-great-1994-bolivia-activity-7445750219536789504-DWUI

3 What if / What might change?

Hypothetical alternative

A plausible alternative would be a traditional, physical globe similar to Princeton's original Geophysical Globe, which was displayed in Guyot Hall for more than 60 years before being donated to a museum. Like the digital globe, it would allow visitors to explore Earth's continents, oceans, and physical features. However, it would not be able to display changing environmental conditions, such as moving ocean currents, weather patterns, temperature changes, or earthquake activity. Without the digital globe, visitors would lose the opportunity to see how Earth's natural systems interact and change over time. While a traditional globe would require no electricity and likely less technological maintenance, it would offer fewer opportunities for interactive learning and scientific exploration.

For the environment: One environmental consequence of choosing the digital globe over a traditional physical globe is increased energy consumption. The digital globe requires electricity to power its projection system and display, while a traditional globe can be viewed without electricity. If that electricity comes from fossil fuels the digital globe could contribute indirectly to greenhouse gas emissions. Additionally, the digital globe requires electronic components that eventually need maintenance or replacement, creating potential electronic waste which may release harmful pollutants into the environment. 2

For people: One major human benefit of the digital globe is that it makes complicated scientific information easier for people to understand. Instead of reading about climate change, earthquakes, or ocean currents in a textbook, visitors can see these processes displayed across an entire globe. This helps students and other visitors recognize how environmental events in one region can affect other parts of the world. According to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), spherical displays are specifically designed to make complex Earth processes easier to understand and encourage environmental education. However, there is also a tradeoff. The digital globe depends on electricity, functioning technology, and regular maintenance. If the projection system stops working or requires repairs, visitors temporarily lose access to this educational resource. Unlike a traditional globe, it cannot provide the same experience without functioning electronic equipment.

What we do not know: One uncertainty in this comparison is how much electricity the digital globe actually uses. I have assumed that it consumes more energy than a traditional physical globe, but I do not know its exact energy consumption or whether Briger Hall uses renewable energy to power it. If the building gets most of its electricity from renewable sources, the globe's environmental impact could be much smaller than expected. Similarly, knowing how often the globe requires repairs or replacement parts would help determine its long-term environmental cost. These details could change how environmentally beneficial a traditional globe would be compared with the digital version.
